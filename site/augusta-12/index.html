Analysis Nº 003

Where to Aim at Augusta's 12th, Pin by Pin

Golden Bell plays 155 yards over Rae's Creek and still wrecks cards every April. I ran the tee shot through the same aim-point model behind release 002: a dispersion oval per handicap tier, priced against the hole's bunkers and water. Two of the three pins are fine to attack at every tier, including the one I expected to punish mid-handicappers. The back-right Sunday pin isn't: bail 5 to 10 yards left and you lose next to nothing.

    Chapter 1

    "Aim for the center" is folklore until it has a number

    Rae's Creek

    Each oval is one standard deviation of that tier's shot pattern at the 155-yard shot, drawn to scale in model yards and centered on the same reference point.

    Golf instruction calls it playing to the fat part of the green. Course-management columns call it taking your medicine. Nobody attaches a number to either phrase, and the advice sounds the same whether the reader carries a 2-handicap or a 22.

    This piece runs the aim decision through the model behind release 002 instead: one verdict per handicap tier, per pin, stated as a line and a club. Three pins guard the 12th at Augusta National. Two of them turn out fine to attack at every tier. One doesn't, and the chapters ahead show why in each case.

    Every golfer's shot pattern around a single target forms a dispersion oval: how far the ball lands long or short, how far it drifts left or right. The oval sets the odds before the swing happens. Widen it, and the same aim point sends more balls into the bunker or the creek.

    The five ovals in the figure belong to the five handicap tiers this piece tracks, all aimed at the same reference point and drawn to the same scale. Scratch's oval runs about 18.7 by 6.2 yards (down-range by left-right); a 20-handicap's runs 33.6 by 11.2, almost double the spread on both axes. The 15 and 20-handicap ovals carry a modeled tag: the proximity data behind them was measured at a shorter distance, so their size at this 155-yard shot comes from an extrapolation, not a direct read. The ratio between the down-range and left-right axes (anisotropy, in the model's own vocabulary) is itself modeled at 3-to-1, with a sensitivity range of 2:1 to 3.5:1 tested throughout this piece.

    The left pin sits mid-depth on the green's roomier lobe, the fat target a course-management column would send a reader to first. The model agrees, at every tier.

    Scratch

    Attack the flag or bail 4 yards toward the center. Either works.

    The model's own aim sits 3.9 yards right of the flag (toward the green's wider middle) and about half a club long, worth 0.047 strokes over playing it dead at the pin, inside the 0.05 tossup line. Water risk falls from 7.8% at the flag to 6.0% at that aim; the odds of finding the green climb from 34.5% to 44.9%.

    10-handicap

    Same read: aim 4.5 yards right and about half a club long. Either works.

    Worth 0.031 strokes over the flag. Water risk stays close either way, 6.5% at the flag against 6.8% at the aim; green odds rise from 24.4% to 31.3%.

    I expected the center pin to demonstrate the glossary's sucker-pin idea: fair for scratch, marginal for a 15. The model found something flatter. At every tier, in calm air, attacking the center pin costs next to nothing.

    Scratch

    Attack it. Aim 1.4 yards left, same club. Either works.

    Worth 0.013 strokes. Water risk moves from 8.0% at the flag to 7.0% at the aim; green odds hold flat, 47.6% to 47.8%.

    10-handicap

    Same call: aim 1.4 yards left, same club. Either works.

    Worth 0.015 strokes. Water risk falls from 7.6% to 5.6%; green odds hold flat, 34.1% to 33.9%.

    15-handicap

    Still a tossup: aim dead on the flag's own line, about half a club less.

    Worth 0.006 strokes, the marginal case I expected and never found. Water risk ticks up from 7.8% to 8.2%; green odds hold flat, 28.1% to 27.7%.

    20-handicap

    The closest thing to a pure tossup on the whole hole: aim 1 yard left, same club.

    Worth 0.002 strokes, about the size of a rounding error. Water risk falls from 6.8% to 6.2%; green odds rise from 22.2% to 23.2%.

    This site's glossary defines a sucker pin as a flag whose direct line sends a meaningful share of the oval into a hazard or a short-sided lie. The center pin's own front-third depth runs about 10.5 yards on average (a modeled range of 9 to 12), enough room that even a 20-handicap's much wider oval spreads its misses across green, rough, and the near edge of the creek without concentrating them anywhere lethal. That isn't what a sucker pin looks like. The one the model finds is next.

    The Sunday pin sits in the green's back-right corner, about 15 yards deeper than a front-left flag and closest to Rae's Creek. Every tier's own verdict says the same thing: bail.

    Scratch

    Bail. Aim 7 yards left of the flag and about one club less.

    Worth 0.093 strokes, well clear of the tossup line. Water risk falls from 12.9% at the flag to 9.8% at that aim; green odds rise from 39.5% to 46.0%.

    10-handicap

    Bail again: aim 8 yards left and about one club less.

    Worth 0.093 strokes. Water risk drops from 10.2% to 8.4%; green odds rise from 27.5% to 34.4%. The model's strict, unclamped search wants to lay up even farther, 13.4 yards short of the flag, but that deeper layup beats the full-shot aim above by 0.011 strokes, inside the tossup line, which is why this piece publishes the full swing.

    15-handicap

    Still bail: aim 5 yards left and about one club less.

    Worth 0.082 strokes. Water risk drops from 9.6% to 7.5%; green odds rise from 23.8% to 28.1%. The strict search wants 17.0 yards of layup here, an edge of 0.014 strokes over the full-shot aim, again inside the tossup line.

    20-handicap

    Bail hardest here: aim 5.6 yards left and about one club less.

    Worth 0.073 strokes. Water risk moves from 8.5% to 8.1%; green odds rise from 18.0% to 22.5%. The strict search now wants 26.9 yards of layup, its biggest edge of any tier, 0.032 strokes, still under the 0.05 tossup line.

    The delta for attacking Sunday sits in a tight band, 0.073 to 0.093 strokes in calm air, at every tier this piece tests: the pin costs every tier close to the same amount. I guessed the higher handicaps would suffer more; the numbers say otherwise. The real gradient shows up one level deeper. The model's strict, unclamped search wants to lay up farther than a full swing reaches for the higher tiers, and how much farther grows with handicap: nothing extra for scratch or the 5-handicap, 0.011 strokes for a 10, 0.014 for a 15, 0.032 for a 20. Every one of those numbers stays inside the tossup line, which is why the verdicts above publish the full-shot aim instead of the deeper layup. But the direction is the finding: the higher the handicap, the more the model wants that golfer standing farther back.

    Chapter 5

    Wind adds eight yards of trouble, not more bail

    Calm oval
    Windy oval
    Rae's Creek

    15-handicap, Sunday pin, published aim in both conditions.

    The source log carries wind at this hole as a narrative fact, not a number (Anchor 6): the tee sits sheltered while the green sits exposed, and the 2019 final round played through gusts of about 20 mph. The model prices wind as two modeled multipliers: an 8-yard carry penalty (range 4 to 12) and a 1.3x dispersion inflation (range 1.15 to 1.5), applied on top of whichever tier and pin the golfer is playing.

    Calm

    A 15-handicap's oval at Sunday measures 28.1 by 9.4 yards, and the bail is worth 0.082 strokes.

    Wind

    The oval swells to 36.5 by 12.2 yards, but the bail signal gets quieter, not louder: 0.057 strokes.

    Still on the bail side of the 0.05 tossup line, less lopsided than calm air. Left and center don't move: every tier's verdict at both pins stays "either works" in the wind this piece tests, the same as calm air.

    Chapter 6

    April 14, 2019: four balls in Rae's Creek within the hour

    In the final round of the 2019 Masters, four contenders found Rae's Creek off the tee at the 12th within about an hour of each other. Brooks Koepka's 9-iron drifted right, caught the bank, and rolled back into the water. Ian Poulter's 8-iron came up short and never carried the creek. Francesco Molinari's tee shot caught the same bank and rolled back into the creek. Tony Finau's tee shot found the water too. All four made double bogey.

    One correction the source hunt caught: a separate Molinari ball found water later that round, deflected off a tree branch during a chip. That shot happened on the par-5 15th, not the 12th, and belongs to a different swing.

    Wind gusted to about 20 mph that afternoon, the kind of push the wind chapter above prices as an 8-yard carry penalty and a wider oval, not a fixed stroke count.

    The week's own numbers show how rare that hour was against how the hole plays most of the time: 2019's tournament-wide scoring average on 12 sat at 3.053, with 52 birdies, 200 pars, 38 bogeys, and 14 double-bogeys-or-worse across 304 plays. One bad hour produced four of those doubles.

    Chapter 7

    Does the model know this hole?

    Per-year scoring averages, hole 12, 2019-2025 (2020 not played). Dashed line: the model's own Tour season mean.

    The amateur ovals come from published amateur proximity data. The Tour oval runs through the same construction, and it is the one the hole's own scoring record can check, so before trusting the amateur verdicts the Tour oval has to reproduce that record.

    At the 155-yard shot, the model's Tour oval measures 8.4 by 5.4 yards, tight enough that Rae's Creek almost never enters a Tour player's equation. Run through a full season of pin positions and wind, the model's Tour season average comes out to 3.1188 strokes.

    That number lands inside the modern-era band this release targets: the mean of six published per-year averages (2019, 2021 through 2025, no usable 2020 figure) runs 3.053 to 3.233 and averages 3.132, with a sample standard deviation of about 0.073. The hole's all-time scoring average, 3.27 to 3.28, describes a different era: it spans tournaments back to 1934, and every modern year on record scores below it. Comparing a model built on 2016-2023 proximity data against a 90-year average would grade this release against a different hole, so ADR 0002 retargets the gate to the modern years instead.

    One gap stays open and disclosed. Matched against 2019's and 2024's own published outcome shares, the model's Tour oval overproduces bogeys and underproduces pars: about six points high on the bogey bucket in both years (5.7 points for 2019, 6.6 points for 2024). The season average checks out; the shot-by-shot shape still runs a little too rough.

    Chapter 10

    Method and sources

    Every oval in this piece comes from the same three-step chain. First, published proximity and greens-in-regulation figures at the 155-yard band get inverted into a radial miss scale per tier, direct for scratch through 10-handicap and interpolated for 15 and 20. Second, that radial scale splits into a down-range axis and a left-right axis using an anisotropy ratio. Third, the resulting oval gets placed against the hole's own geometry, bunker positions, the green's diagonal shoe-sole shape, and Rae's Creek's own finite band, and integrated over every outcome region to price expected strokes.

    A flip set sweeps every verdict across the anisotropy range and the front-third-depth range together, at each pin, tier, and wind state, and records which qualitative labels change. The Sunday pin never flips inside that rectangle: it reads "bail" at every tier and wind state, at every sensitivity setting tested. Left and center sit closer to the tossup line and move at a few corners:

    • Scratch's left pin flips from "either works" to "bail" at the widest anisotropy tested (3.5:1), in both calm air and wind.
    • Scratch's center pin flips from "either works" to "bail" at the narrowest anisotropy (2.0:1), under wind but not calm.
    • The 5-handicap's left pin flips the other direction, from "bail" at the sweep's own baseline to "either works" at its calm, narrow-anisotropy corner.

    None of these flips touch the piece's central finding: the Sunday pin's "bail" verdict is the one result that survives every sensitivity setting tested, at every tier.
