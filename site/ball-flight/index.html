Analysis Nº 004

Face, path, and the ball flight laws, in numbers

Instructors teach two rules. The face sets the start line, and the gap between face and path sets the curve. I fitted a flight model to TrackMan's published Tour averages and put a number on each rule for the driver, the 6-iron and the wedge. The figures below run that model in your browser, so every number here matches the Ball flight lab.

Golfers hear the ball flight laws as slogans. Attack angle joins the two rules above, since it changes launch and spin and drags the path along with it. I wanted the weights: how much of the start line the face owns, how many yards a degree bends a wedge against a driver, and what the numbers read for a Tour pro, an LPGA player and a 14.5-handicap.

Published numbers come from TrackMan and PING and cite their pages. Model numbers carry the modeled badge and are computed live.

Chapter 1

Face sets the start line

Open the Tour driver's face 4 degrees with the path at zero, and the ball starts 3.1 degrees right of the target. Leave the face square and swing the path 4 degrees right, and the start line moves 0.9 degree. Figure 1 shows both moves for the driver and the 7-iron.

That split puts the face's share of the start direction at 79 percent for the driver and 73 percent for the 6-iron. The driver's share runs higher because the ball leaves closer to where the face points as spin loft drops. I fitted the model to TrackMan's published dynamic loft and launch angle for the Tour driver and 6-iron, so these shares are model output.

You may have heard 85 percent for the driver and 75 for irons, or 87 and 81. I could not trace either pair to a TrackMan text. A 2014 coaching-academy page gives the first pair and names no source. A forum post gives the second and cites TrackMan Academy without a link. TrackMan's own pages call the face the main contributor to launch direction and print no percentage. My driver share sits 6 points under the 85 and my 6-iron share sits 2 under the 75. That comparison checks plausibility and cannot pick a winner. The table that should settle it sits in Fredrik Tuxen's TrackMan Ball Flight Laws paper, which I could not open. Until someone reads it, treat every share as an estimate.

Chapter 2

Face-to-path sets the curve

Face-to-path is the face angle minus the club path. The ball curves toward the face and away from the path, and the club decides how far. At 2 degrees of face-to-path the Tour driver in the model bends 18.0 yards, the 6-iron 10.4 and the pitching wedge 3.1. The driver bends 5.8 times as far as the wedge and 1.7 times as far as the 6-iron.

The gap comes from spin loft. The Tour driver has 14.3 degrees of it and the wedge 39. Divide face-to-path by spin loft and you land close to the tilt of the spin axis, so a degree of face-to-path tips a driver's axis far more than a wedge's. The driver also flies farther, which gives the curve more room to build.

TrackMan's eight worked examples anchor the driver and the 6-iron. The lines pass within 5.4 yards of all eight dots. TrackMan published no wedge example, so the wedge line is an extrapolation.

Chapter 3

Attack angle changes launch, spin, and path

Attack angle changes spin loft, which is dynamic loft minus attack angle. Hold the Tour driver's dynamic loft at 13.4 degrees and swing down 6 degrees, and the model launches the ball 8.8 degrees high with 3,684 rpm on 19.4 degrees of spin loft. Swing up 6 degrees and the same loft launches at 11.9 degrees with 1,086 rpm on 7.4 degrees.

Read the dotted segments with care. The launch fit has no data below 12.7 degrees of spin loft, so the model extrapolates there. The optimizer charts raise dynamic loft as attack angle rises and the figure holds it fixed, which is why the model's spin runs under both charts at positive attack angles.

Attack angle moves the path too. Instructors teach that hitting down pushes the path right of where a shallower swing would put it, and the geometry agrees. With the swing direction held, each extra degree of down-attack adds about 0.87 degree of path on a 49-degree swing plane, the Combine average for a driver. The formula comes from a 2011 Brian Manzella Golf forum post. TrackMan says path comes from swing direction, swing plane and attack angle and prints no equation.

Steepen the Tour driver by 4 degrees and hold the swing direction and a square face. The path moves 3.5 degrees right. The ball starts 0.8 degree right of target, launches at 9.2 degrees with 3,508 rpm, and hooks 28 yards.

Chapter 4

The ideal window by club

The lab shades an ideal band on every tile. TrackMan publishes Tour averages for every club and no target launch or spin for irons, so the Tour rows serve as the ideal for the Tour presets. The average-amateur rows stand on less: a measured driver row from TrackMan's Combine data, Optimizer defaults for the 6-iron and wedge, and modeled numbers for everything else.

The driver has two published optimizers: TrackMan's 2010 driver fitting charts and PING's 2019 launch and spin chart. Figure 4 sets each group's published average against both, at that group's speed and attack angle. The lab's band spans the two charts and adds 1 degree of launch and 200 rpm of spin.

The PGA Tour numbers agree with the charts: the average driver launches at 10.4 degrees against 9.3 from TrackMan and 10.4 from PING. The LPGA Tour average does not agree. LPGA players launch the driver at 12.6 degrees on a 2.8-degree up-swing. TrackMan's optimizer asks for 14.2 at that speed and attack angle and PING's chart asks for 14.8, so the LPGA average launches 1.6 to 2.2 degrees below both. That gap sits in TrackMan's published averages and does not depend on my model.

Two cautions apply. The charts date from 2010 and 2019 and the LPGA table from 2023, and each chart optimizes carry for its own ball and conditions. A fitter could start with that gap. The average amateur launches at 12.6 degrees with 3,275 rpm, which matches TrackMan's spin and sits 510 rpm over PING's.

Chapter 5

Tiger's nine windows

Nine windows is a drill Tiger Woods made part of his routine: three trajectories, low, mid and high, each shaped as a draw, a straight shot and a fade. TaylorMade's 2021 video "Tiger Woods' Nine Windows" carries his walkthrough with his irons, and its description calls the drill an example of the control a golfer can have with an iron. I cite the video for the concept alone.

No one has published launch monitor numbers for the nine windows, so I built the recipes. For each window a solver finds the path, face, attack angle and dynamic loft that hit a target peak height and curve with the Tour 7-iron. The low and high windows peak at 70 and 125 percent of the standard shot's height, and the draw and fade bend 5 percent of carry. Figure 5 flies all nine.

Height comes from loft and attack angle together. The straight low window takes 15.3 degrees of dynamic loft and a 7.1-degree down-swing, and carries 186 yards with a 27-yard peak. The mid window takes 23.4 degrees and 3.9 down for 174 and 38. The high window takes 34.6 degrees and 0.6 up for 152 and 48.

Shape follows the same pattern at every height. The mid draw wants the path 4.4 degrees right with the face 2.3 degrees right, a face-to-path of −2.1 that bends the ball 9 yards and brings it back to the target line. The lab's 9 Windows mode flies the same nine recipes.

Chapter 6

How to use the lab with a student

  1. Set the student's miss. Pick the club and the closest player row, then enter the path and face from the student's launch monitor session. An average-amateur driver with a −4 path and a square face is a slice: it starts on line, bends 22 yards and finishes 19 yards right. If that matches the range, you share a picture.
  2. Pin it, then change one variable. Switch to Compare mode, pin this shot, and change the path alone, from −4 to −2. The curve falls to 11 yards, one cause and one effect.
  3. Fix the start line first. The face carries about 79 percent of the driver's start direction, so point the face where the ball should start, then use the path to shape the flight.
  4. Trade attack angle for loft. Change attack angle with dynamic loft locked and watch launch and spin move, as in Figure 3. Then restore the launch with loft.
  5. Send the link. The lab's address holds the whole setup: club, player, speed, attack angle, path, face and loft. Copy it, text it, and the student opens the same shot on a phone.

Method and limitsWhat the model is and where it misses

A three-dimensional point mass flies under gravity, drag and Magnus lift, integrated with RK4 at 0.01 seconds in standard sea-level air. Lift and drag follow a quadratic form in spin factor. Its seven coefficients were fitted once to all 23 Tour rows (PGA 12, LPGA 11), with no per-club terms, and spin decays at the published rate from Smits and Smith. A second model turns club speed, attack angle, path, face and dynamic loft into launch, spin and ball speed through the D-plane geometry. The record of both fits is ADR 0004 in the project repository.

  • Calibration tolerance. No single set of coefficients fits every Tour row inside the original gate (carry within 3 percent, height within 3 yards, land angle within 2 degrees). The model passes it on 6 of 23 rows and passes a teaching tolerance (5 percent, 4 yards, 3 degrees) on 12. The PGA 3-wood, 5-wood, hybrid and 6-iron fly 4.5 to 5.1 yards too high, the PGA 3-iron through 5-iron land 3.7 to 4.2 degrees shallower than the table, and the PGA wedge carries 8.5 yards short.
  • Launch, spin and ball speed. Within 1 degree of launch, 10 percent of spin and 2 percent of ball speed, 17 of 23 rows pass. Before the spin trim, the LPGA driver reads 24.5 percent low on spin and the LPGA 3-wood 30 percent high.
  • Spin trims. Each preset multiplies spin by one fixed factor, from 0.77 to 1.32, so the preset reproduces its published spin to within 1 percent. The factor stays as you move the sliders. The LPGA driver (1.32) and LPGA 3-wood (0.77) mark where the model is weakest.
  • Modeled amateur irons. The amateur driver is the one measurement. The 6-iron and wedge are Optimizer defaults, and every other amateur club interpolates between those anchors.
  • Curvature. The model reproduces the eight TrackMan examples within 20 percent or 3 yards in calibration. It runs 2 to 12 percent short of the carries those examples quote, and it bends the ball the same amount left and right where TrackMan's driver examples are a little asymmetric. Every other club extrapolates from the fit.
  • Start-direction shares. Unverified against a primary TrackMan text, as Chapter 1 says.
  • Low spin loft. The launch fit spans 12.7 to 25.9 degrees of spin loft. Outside it, including a driver hit up more than a degree or two at Tour loft, the model extrapolates.
  • Roll. Bounce and roll come from a simple empirical rule with no published source, so total distance is modeled.
  • Averages. Tour tables pool competition and range shots and give one average per club. The model reproduces an average shot and has no dispersion.
  • Conventions. TrackMan reports club speed, attack angle and path at the center of the club head, and camera-based monitors read attack angle about 2 degrees higher. The sliders follow TrackMan. TrackMan's tables do not adjust for altitude or weather.
  • Nine windows. The recipes are model output, and no primary source publishes numbers for any window.
